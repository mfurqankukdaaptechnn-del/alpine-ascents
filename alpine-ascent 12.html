01 · The Discipline

What mountaineering is, and everything that goes into it

Mountaineering — also called mountain climbing — is the sport of attaining, or attempting to attain, high points in mountainous terrain. Unlike most sports, it has no universal rulebook: climbers adopt a wide range of techniques and philosophies depending on the mountain, the season, and the style they choose. What follows are the five pillars every climber has to understand before roping up.

A short history of mountaineering

Mountaineering as an organised pursuit is younger than most people assume. For most of human history mountains were feared, avoided, or climbed only for religious and survival reasons. That changed in the late 18th century in the European Alps, and accelerated through two centuries of exploration, nationalism, and eventually pure sport.

1786

Birth of modern mountaineering

Jacques Balmat and Michel-Gabriel Paccard make the first recorded ascent of Mont Blanc, widely regarded as the founding moment of the sport.

1857

The Alpine Club founded

The world's first mountaineering club is established in London, formalising climbing as an organised activity with shared ethics and record-keeping.

1865

The Matterhorn is climbed

Edward Whymper's ascent — and the fatal descent that followed — closes what's called the "Golden Age of Alpinism" in the Alps.

1953

Everest's summit is reached

Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay become the first confirmed climbers to stand on the highest point on Earth.

1954

K2 falls to an Italian expedition

Achille Compagnoni and Lino Lacedelli complete the first ascent of K2, the hardest of the eight-thousanders, from Pakistan's Karakoram range.

1978

Climbing without bottled oxygen

Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler prove Everest can be climbed without supplemental oxygen, reshaping what "fair means" climbing looks like.

2021

K2's first winter ascent

A ten-member Nepali team completes the first winter summit of K2, closing the last of the eight-thousanders' winter-ascent list.

Types & styles of mountaineering

Climbers don't just choose a mountain — they choose a style, and the style changes everything about the risk, the gear, and the timeline.

Style

Alpine Style

Fast, lightweight, self-supported ascents with no fixed camps or porters — climb continuously, carry everything yourself.

Style

Expedition Style

Slow and siege-based: fixed ropes, multiple stocked camps, and support teams ferrying supplies up the mountain over weeks.

Style

High-Altitude Mountaineering

Climbing above roughly 5,500m, where the body cannot fully acclimatise and supplemental oxygen is often used above 8,000m.

Style

Ice & Mixed Climbing

Ascending frozen waterfalls, glaciers, and rock-ice combinations using ice tools and crampons on steep terrain.

Style

Ski Mountaineering

Climbing with skis attached, or carried, to descend technical alpine terrain rather than walking down.

Style

Big Wall & Free Solo

Multi-day vertical rock ascents using aid or free climbing, at the far end without a rope at all — the highest-risk style in the sport.

Core techniques

Technique is what turns a mountain from a hazard into a route. These are the fundamentals taught before anyone touches an 8,000m peak.

Roped Travel

Climbers tie into a shared rope on glaciers so a fall into a crevasse can be arrested by the rest of the team.

Self-Arrest

Using an ice axe to stop a slide on snow or ice before it becomes a fall — the first skill taught on any snow course.

Cramponing

Footwork on steel spikes for ice and hard snow, from flat-footed "French technique" to front-pointing on steep faces.

Belaying & Rappelling

Managing rope tension to protect a climbing partner, and controlled rope descents to retreat from terrain that can't be down-climbed.

Acclimatisation

Climbing high, sleeping low — a staged ascent pattern that lets the body adapt to thinning oxygen before pushing for the summit.

Fixed Rope & Jumaring

Ropes anchored to the mountain that climbers ascend using a mechanical clamp, standard on expedition-style 8,000m routes.

Sheltering on the mountain

Above base camp, shelter is a survival system, not a comfort. Expeditions build a ladder of camps up the mountain, each one stocked and used only as long as needed before the summit push.

  • Base Camp — the expedition's operating hub, often reachable by trek
  • Advanced Base Camp (ABC) — the first high-altitude staging point
  • Camps I–IV — progressively higher, smaller, more exposed tent sites
  • Summit push — often launched from the highest camp at night

Four-Season Tents

Reinforced dome tents built to hold shape in high wind and heavy snow load.

Snow Caves

Dug into a snowbank for insulation and wind protection when no tent is available.

Igloos

Blocks of compacted snow built into a dome — slower to build, but excellent insulation.

Bivouac (Bivy)

A minimal sleeping-bag-and-shell setup used for emergency or fast-and-light overnight stops.

Hazards — the honest list

Mountaineering is, for the untrained, genuinely dangerous. These are the hazards that account for most serious incidents.

Terrain

Avalanches

Sudden snow-slope failure — the leading cause of mountaineering deaths on snow-covered peaks.

Terrain

Crevasses

Hidden glacier cracks, often snow-bridged and invisible, crossed only with roped travel.

Physiology

Altitude Sickness (AMS / HACE / HAPE)

Reduced oxygen at altitude causing anything from headaches to fatal fluid build-up in the brain or lungs.

Terrain

Rockfall & Icefall

Loose rock or collapsing seracs above the route — largely time-of-day and route-choice dependent.

Physiology

Frostbite & Hypothermia

Cold injury to extremities and dropping core temperature, both accelerated by wind and wet clothing.

Weather

Whiteouts & Storms

Sudden loss of visibility and violent weather windows that can strand climbers above their last safe camp.

06 · Latest Developments

What's changing in the sport right now

2025

Nanga Parbat's first winter ascent finally falls — after three winters of trying

Nanga Parbat, the "Killer Mountain," resisted winter ascent longer than any other Pakistani 8,000er. A team's three-year push through repeated storm cycles and turnarounds finally put climbers on the summit in winter conditions, closing out one of the Karakoram's last great winter-climbing objectives.

2024

Satellite communicators become standard kit

Two-way satellite messengers (Garmin inReach, Zoleo) are now standard expedition gear, letting climbers send SOS signals and weather updates from anywhere on the mountain, independent of cell coverage.

2024

Glacial retreat is reshaping classic routes

Warming is exposing bare ice and rockfall on routes once covered in stable snow, pushing guiding services to re-survey approach routes on peaks like the Matterhorn and Mont Blanc almost every season.

2023–24

Karakoram permit reform

Pakistan's tourism authorities have streamlined expedition permit processing for K2 and neighbouring Gasherbrum peaks, cutting paperwork time and expanding porter-employment programs around Skardu.

Ongoing

Lighter, smarter gear

Advances in insulation fabrics and battery-heated boot liners are cutting summit-day pack weight, extending the safe window climbers have at extreme altitude.

Ongoing

Sustainable expedition practice

"Leave No Trace" waste-carry-down policies are now enforced by permit on most 8,000m peaks, with fixed penalties for abandoned oxygen bottles and tents.

07 · General Guidelines

Before you rope up

General, non-technical guidance for anyone considering mountaineering. This is not a substitute for certified training or a guided course.

01

Build a base before you go high

Learn roped travel, self-arrest, and navigation on low-risk terrain before attempting any glaciated or high-altitude peak.

02

Climb with a certified guide or club

Especially for a first expedition — local knowledge of route conditions and weather patterns is not optional.

03

Acclimatise, don't rush

Follow a staged ascent profile; altitude illness is unpredictable and unrelated to fitness level.

04

Check weather and objective hazards daily

Avalanche and rockfall risk change hour to hour — a route safe at dawn may not be safe by noon.

05

Always have a turnaround time

Set it before you leave camp, and respect it regardless of how close the summit looks.

06

Carry redundant communication & navigation

A satellite communicator, map, compass/GPS, and a known emergency contact plan, every time.

07

Respect permits, porters, and local communities

Follow the host country's climbing regulations and pay porters and guides fairly — the mountain community sustains the sport.

World Record Holders

The climbers who set the bar

The people whose ascents redefined what's possible at altitude — worth studying before you set any goal of your own.

Italy

Reinhold Messner

First to summit Everest without supplemental oxygen (1978, with Peter Habeler), and first to climb all 14 eight-thousanders (1986).

Nepal

Nirmal "Nims" Purja

Climbed all 14 eight-thousanders in 6 months 6 days in 2019 — "Project Possible" — a feat once assumed to be decades away.

Japan

Junko Tabei

First woman to summit Everest (1975), and later the first woman to complete the Seven Summits.

Pakistan

Sirbaz Khan

First Pakistani to climb all 14 eight-thousanders (2023), then the first Pakistani to complete all 14 without supplemental oxygen (2025).

Pakistan

Naila Kiani

First Pakistani woman to summit all five of Pakistan's 8,000m peaks, building one of the country's fastest-growing mountaineering records.

Nepal

Kami Rita Sherpa

Holds the record for the most Everest summits by any climber — a career built on decades of guiding on the mountain.
